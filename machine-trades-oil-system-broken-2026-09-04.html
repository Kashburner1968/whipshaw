THE MACHINE TRADES THE OIL; THE SYSTEM IS BROKEN

Computer algorithms are responsible for as much as 70% of crude-oil trades on an average day.
Faultline Financial · September 4, 2026

Oil is an unpredictable and volatile business. War causes shipping disruptions. Sanctions create unstable populations desperate for food, medicine, and gas to power their cars.

A damaged port, a threatened shipping lane, a refinery outage, or an embargo can change the physical oil market quickly. Yet computer algorithms are responsible for as much as 70% of crude-oil trades on an average day.

Commodity Trading Advisors, or CTAs, are major players. These computer-driven strategies have accounted for nearly 60% of net trading volume within managed money by some measures. Many respond to political rhetoric, defying conventional macroeconomic logic.

That matters.

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller suggests the central bank remains open to holding rates steady. President Trump aggressively amplifies this shift by publicly demanding the Fed slash interest rates under threat of trade retaliation against deficit nations.

At the same time, the political language surrounding the war changes. However, declaring that a war is over tells the bots there is less risk involved.

The price of oil drops.

Some disruptions are intermittent. Some reports are preliminary. Supply estimates fluctuate, but the consequences of war on the oil market remain uncertain.

The physical risk does not disappear because someone declared on social media that the war is over. Statements like this carry far more weight when they come from the President of the United States than from an ordinary citizen scanning groceries for a living.

The reality is that tankers still have to move through mined waters. Ships still need expensive insurance. Ports still have to operate amid missiles being fired and bombs being dropped. Refineries still need the crude that is arriving more slowly and at much lower levels.

The physical world did not change because the President declared the war over.

But the CTAs are pricing oil as if it did.

The tankers are still moving through dangerous waters. The infrastructure remains vulnerable. The supply constraints remain. Yet the financial price moves as though the danger has passed.

The war is not over. The machines are trading as if it is.

This is where the price begins feeding the machine. One algorithm reacts to the political rhetoric. Another sees oil falling and reads the price movement as confirmation. Other automated systems join the move. Volatility models adjust. Machines begin reacting to machines.

The headline becomes a price move. The price move becomes another signal. The signal generates another trade.

Nothing had to change in the physical oil market.

If machines dominate the trading that establishes the financial price of crude, and their programming tells them there is less risk when they read statements such as “I wouldn't call it a war,” then we need to ask a basic question: how much of the price on the screen represents physical oil—and how much represents machines reacting to headlines, price, political rhetoric, and other machines?

The autobots have made fundamental analysis of the oil business feel like a wasted task at the exact moment it is desperately needed. Analysts can study production, inventories, tanker traffic, refinery capacity, shipping routes, insurance costs, sanctions and actual supply. Then a political statement hits the screen. The bots read less risk. Oil falls.

The fundamentals did not become irrelevant. The machines made the price behave as if they were.

False or purposefully hopeful statements that ease fears about the risks of war can keep the market from correcting toward fair value after prices have already risen quickly on an equally hopeful narrative.

The machines trade the change in sentiment. Less war. Less risk. Lower oil. Lower inflation expectations. Less pressure on rates. Higher stock valuations. But none of that requires the physical conditions to improve.

Hope raises the market. More hope prevents the correction.

When algorithms repeatedly price political optimism faster than the physical economy can confirm it, fundamental analysis loses control of price discovery. That is how an overvalued market can remain overvalued while the risks underneath it continue to grow.

Oil is not just another financial asset. Oil becomes gasoline and diesel. Diesel moves trucks, tractors and construction equipment. Transportation moves food and manufactured goods. Energy costs reach household budgets, corporate margins and inflation. Inflation reaches the Federal Reserve. Interest rates reach mortgages, business financing and stock valuations.

A computer can reprice crude in milliseconds. A tanker cannot move faster because an algorithm changed its risk score. A refinery cannot produce more gasoline because a headline changed. A farmer's diesel bill does not disappear because a bot decided geopolitical risk had fallen.

The machine trades the signal. The economy pays the price.

This practice needs to stop. Regulation needs to happen. Markets cannot continue allowing automated systems to amplify political rhetoric, suppress fundamental price discovery, and push valuations farther from the physical economy without eventually paying a price.

The danger is that the correction does not happen gradually. Risk accumulates beneath the price. Fundamentals deteriorate while algorithms continue trading optimism. Then something happens that the models cannot dismiss, reinterpret, or outrun. The narrative breaks. Everyone tries to leave at once.

That is the Black Swan risk.

The same machines that helped prevent the correction can accelerate the panic when it finally arrives. Oil moves. Yields move. Volatility explodes. Liquidity disappears. An overvalued market suddenly discovers the fundamentals it spent months ignoring.

By then, regulation is too late.
