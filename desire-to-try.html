AI, Human Experience, and the Desire to Try

A conversation about process, mastery, discovery, innovation, work, and what is lost when the outcome becomes easier than the experience that gave it meaning.

Context: Elon Musk's G20 argument

This argument was prompted in part by Elon Musk's remarks at the G20 Innovation Ministerial in North Carolina on September 1, 2026. Musk presented a highly optimistic economic case for rapid AI deployment while also making unusually short-horizon predictions about human competitiveness in digital work.

Musk estimated that digital AI could increase the global economy by roughly 20% to 30%, or on the order of $20 trillion to $30 trillion per year. He also predicted that AI would be able to do essentially anything digital that does not require physically shaping atoms by the end of 2027.

His software prediction is especially important to the argument on this page. Musk compared future AI programming capability to Stockfish, the chess engine that can defeat the strongest human chess players. He predicted that sometime next year AI software would become “Stockfish-level” good, meaning that, in his view, a human would no longer be able to compete with AI at writing software. He extended the same short horizon more broadly, saying AI would become extremely capable across engineering and digital work within roughly 12 to 18 months.

Musk paired this with an even larger robotics forecast: he argued that humanoid robots could eventually multiply global economic output many times over, and he predicted more than a billion humanoid robots within about a decade. His overall case was one of extraordinary abundance, productivity, and economic expansion.

This page asks what that economic argument leaves out. If people hear that the skill they are beginning to learn may become noncompetitive within 12 to 18 months, the effect is not confined to a future labor market. The prediction can change motivation now. A person can stop studying, practicing, painting, programming, researching, or trying before the predicted capability even arrives. The central question is therefore not whether Musk's forecast ultimately proves right or wrong. It is what happens when a near-term forecast of human obsolescence becomes part of the environment in which people decide whether acquiring a skill is still worth the effort.

The contrast is fundamental: Musk's argument measures AI primarily through output, productivity, abundance, and economic growth. The argument here focuses on what those measurements do not capture: process, mastery, participation, curiosity, discovery, motivation, and the human desire to try.

Source context: Musk's G20 remarks were reported by Barron's and Yahoo Finance/Stocktwits and are available in published transcripts. Contemporary reporting also notes that the broader G20 technology meeting emphasized AI adoption and relatively light regulation. These are Musk's predictions, not established timelines or guarantees.

The central issue

The central concern is not simply whether AI can produce better outputs. The concern is what happens to the desire to learn, practice, struggle, discover, create, and master something when people are told that within 12–18 months a machine will be vastly better at the very activity they are trying to learn.

A distant prediction leaves room for a lifetime of mastery. A 12–18 month prediction says, in effect: why even start? The prediction can change behavior in the present before anyone knows whether the prediction is true.

AI does not have to replace a person first. The belief that it soon will can extinguish the desire to try before the promised technology arrives.

The process

Chess

The Stockfish analogy is not abstract. Once computers became so much better at chess that the challenge felt trivialized, the enjoyment disappeared. The loss was not merely the pleasure of winning. The entire process lost its meaning: learning, experimenting, anticipating, failing, improving, discovering, and finally getting something right. That is why Musk's own comparison between future coding AI and Stockfish matters: machine superiority can change the desire to participate even before a skill is literally impossible to perform.

Coding

Learning Java, BASIC, Perl, Python, and HTML was not merely a way to obtain finished software. The value was learning how to make it, solving problems, understanding why something failed, and developing mastery. Against that background, a prediction that AI programming will reach “Stockfish-level” within 12–18 months does more than forecast productivity. It changes the perceived value of beginning the learning process now. Supervising AI is a different activity from becoming capable of building the thing oneself.

Art

The value of painting and drawing was not merely possessing a finished image. It was observing, choosing, struggling, experimenting, developing technique, creating, and eventually producing something through one's own ability.

Math, physics, science, and research

The value is in figuring something out: confronting a problem, thinking, experimenting, failing, revising an idea, and finally understanding something that was not understood before. If AI removes trial and error, it removes a fundamental way we discover.

Innovation and the desire to try

Innovation does not begin with an output. It begins with curiosity and the desire to try. People wonder, experiment, fail, notice anomalies, pursue strange ideas, learn from mistakes, and continue even without a guarantee of success.

Efficiency asks how to make today's thing faster or cheaper. Innovation asks what might exist that nobody has thought of yet.

The capability doesn't have to disappear first. The desire will disappear first.

Musk's G20 argument assumes that rapidly improving AI will accelerate prosperity and productivity. But that acceleration also assumes that people continue entering fields, becoming experts, asking questions, and supplying the curiosity from which new problems and discoveries arise. If short-horizon predictions discourage that process, the innovation pipeline can be affected before automation is complete.

Work, income, and participation

If AI genuinely performs everything a worker provides to a company faster, cheaper, and at higher quality, the economic reason to employ that worker disappears. If labor stops being the primary way people earn a claim on society's output, something else will have to replace the paycheck.

This is where the G20 abundance argument and the participation argument diverge. Even if AI really does add tens of trillions of dollars to global output, aggregate abundance does not determine who receives the gains, nor does it answer what role people occupy when their labor is no longer economically necessary.

Income is only one problem. Giving people enough money to consume does not restore the experience of creating, discovering, mastering, contributing, or being needed. There are two AI problems: distribution and participation.

Output versus process

Painting is not merely obtaining a picture. Math is not merely obtaining an answer. Physics is not merely receiving a theory. Chess is not merely determining a winner. Coding is not merely obtaining software. Research is not merely obtaining findings.

“AI can do it for you” answers the wrong question when the purpose was never merely to obtain the output. The purpose was to experience doing it.
