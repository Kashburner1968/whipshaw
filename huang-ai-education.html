Jensen Huang, AI, and the Young Mind

A challenge to the idea that the worst AI outcome is being left behind — and a warning about what may be lost when AI enters education before a young person has learned how to think without it.

Context and accuracy: At the September 2, 2026 G20 Innovation Ministerial in Chapel Hill, NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang urged countries to accelerate AI adoption, described AI as infrastructure comparable to water, roads, electricity and the internet, and said the “single worst outcome” for a country would be failing to take advantage of the technology and being left behind. He also argued against regulating hypothetical or theoretical harms in favor of actual, pragmatic harms. This essay challenges the educational implications of that worldview. It does not claim Huang used the exact phrase “AI will infiltrate education.”

Huang's argument

Huang's G20 message was fundamentally an acceleration argument: AI is becoming essential infrastructure, countries must build around it, and the greatest danger is failing to move fast enough. In his broader 2026 remarks to students, he has also described AI as the most powerful technology available to a new generation and encouraged graduates to become expert AI users.

But education is not simply another sector waiting to be made more efficient. It is the developmental process by which a person becomes capable of thinking.

If AI enters every stage of that process too early and too deeply, the central risk is not merely cheating, plagiarism, or bad answers. The deeper risk is that a child may learn to obtain the products of thinking before developing the capacity to think independently.

The mistake in treating education like infrastructure

Roads, electricity, water, and computing infrastructure help people do things. Education is different. Education forms the person who will do them. A child is not a finished worker waiting for productivity tools. A child's mind is still being built through reading, recall, frustration, trial and error, sustained attention, explanation, memory, argument, imagination, and the experience of being wrong.

If AI routinely performs those operations before they are internalized, it can remove the very cognitive resistance through which intellectual ability develops.

Performance is not learning

The OECD's 2026 Digital Education Outlook draws a crucial distinction: generative AI can improve the quality of a student's immediate output without producing corresponding learning gains. The OECD warns that offloading cognitive tasks to general-purpose chatbots can produce disengagement, “metacognitive laziness,” and weaker long-term skill acquisition when AI use is not deliberately structured around pedagogy.

This means a student can appear more capable while becoming less independently capable. The essay gets better. The homework becomes cleaner. The answer arrives faster. But when the AI is removed, the underlying knowledge may not be there.

The danger is not that the machine gives the child a wrong answer. The danger is that the machine gives the child the right answer before the child has built the mind required to reach it.

Cognitive offloading before cognitive formation

Adults already possess years of accumulated language, memory, judgment, and reasoning. A young person does not. For an adult, AI may sometimes function as leverage applied to an existing cognitive structure. For a child, the same tool can become a substitute for building that structure in the first place.

Research published in 2026 increasingly distinguishes between AI as a scaffold and AI as a substitute. Reviews of higher-education research find that structured use can support critical thinking, while unstructured dependence is associated with cognitive offloading, reduced analytical autonomy, and weaker epistemic engagement. The lesson for younger learners should be more cautious, not less: foundational capacities should exist before they are routinely outsourced.

The young mind needs friction

Learning requires forms of difficulty that look inefficient from a productivity perspective. Remembering without assistance. Writing a weak first draft. Solving the problem incorrectly. Sitting with confusion. Searching for a word. Building an argument. Discovering a contradiction. Re-reading. Revising. Trying again.

These are not defects in education. They are education.

If an AI system predicts the next sentence, supplies the argument, summarizes the chapter, solves the equation, generates the hypothesis, corrects the grammar, and explains the concept instantly, it can remove the interval in which the student's own mind would otherwise have been forced to act.

What could be damaged

The most credible concern is not literal brain injury. It is developmental displacement: the erosion or underdevelopment of habits and capacities that normally grow through repeated mental effort.

Those capacities include sustained attention, working through uncertainty, memory retrieval, independent composition, frustration tolerance, intellectual confidence, curiosity, self-correction, judgment, and the ability to distinguish understanding from fluent-sounding language.

A child who becomes accustomed to immediate cognitive rescue may become less practiced at remaining inside a difficult problem long enough to transform confusion into understanding.

Agency can disappear quietly

The greatest educational danger may be difficult to measure because the student can still produce excellent work. AI can conceal declining agency behind improving output.

A student may gradually stop asking: What do I think? What do I remember? How would I solve this? What is wrong with my argument? Instead the question becomes: What should I ask the machine?

That is a profound shift. The learner moves from being the originator of thought to the manager of generated thought.

Education should not train a young person to supervise intelligence before it has trained that person to possess intellectual independence.

Huang's “theoretical harm” standard is weakest where children are concerned

Huang argued at the G20 that regulation should focus on actual and pragmatic harms rather than hypothetical or theoretical ones. That may sound sensible in many commercial contexts. But childhood development presents a different problem: by the time a developmental harm becomes undeniable, a cohort may already have spent years forming habits around the technology.

Education necessarily acts under uncertainty. Schools prohibit or limit many things not because every harm has already been conclusively measured across a generation, but because children are still developing and because some losses are difficult to reverse.

The standard cannot simply be: deploy first, wait for incontrovertible damage, regulate later.

The asymmetry of risk

If AI is introduced too slowly, students can learn it later. If foundational reasoning, writing, memory, attention, and self-reliance are weakened during critical developmental years, those capacities may be much harder to rebuild later.

That makes education fundamentally different from an industrial race. A country can buy more GPUs next year. A child cannot return to age nine and reconstruct the exact years in which independent cognitive habits were supposed to form.

A better principle

The choice is not between banning AI and surrendering education to it. A more defensible principle is developmental sequencing.

Students should first learn to read, write, reason, calculate, remember, explain, and create with their own minds. Then AI can be introduced selectively as a tool that challenges, extends, critiques, or accelerates abilities that already exist.

This is close to the OECD's own recommendation that education preserve independent thinking and foundational skills, and that students learn both without AI and with deliberately designed educational AI rather than simply offloading work to general-purpose systems.

Before we teach a child how to use artificial intelligence, we should make sure we have given that child enough time to develop intelligence that is genuinely their own.

The deeper challenge to Huang

The worst outcome is not necessarily that a country is “left behind.” A country can lead technologically while weakening the intellectual independence of the generation that inherits that technology.

A society can become more computationally powerful while its citizens become less practiced at thinking without computational assistance. It can produce better student work while producing less capable independent students. It can measure educational efficiency while quietly removing the struggle through which a mind becomes strong.

If AI becomes unavoidable infrastructure, education should be the place where its use is most deliberate, most age-sensitive, and most constrained by the developmental needs of the learner — not the place where adoption is accelerated simply because the rest of the economy is accelerating.
