I HAVE A DEGREE IN AGRICULTURE. THIS SCARES ME MORE THAN A MARKET CRASH.

A market can recover. An economy can be rebuilt. But when the astronomical cost of diesel combines with an El Niño trend and a weakened agricultural support system, the thing at risk is no longer merely the ups and downs of the price of oil. It is the scarcity of food.
Faultline Financial · September 4, 2026

I have a degree in agriculture from Auburn University. Food does not begin at the grocery store. Food begins with rich soil, good weather and hardy seeds. The harvest begins with fertilizer, machinery, fuel, labor and time.

A financial crash destroys wealth, an economic crash destroys income, and both cause human suffering. But the supply of food sits underneath the financial system and the economy like a strongman holding up our GDP under a circus tent.

Human beings need to eat.

You can create money and lower interest rates. You can recapitalize a bank, reopen a stock exchange, and stimulate an economy. But you can't reprice a food supply once it has been harvested with diesel at today's prices.

You cannot reprice the harvest after the fuel has been burned.

The price of oil is not the number that scares me

Everyone keeps watching oil; I am watching diesel. Crude can stabilize. Crude can fall. More oil can reach the market. But even if all of that happens tomorrow, there is no escaping what the current cost of diesel is going to do to the cost of food in the coming months.

U.S. diesel has climbed to extraordinary levels while the price of diesel relative to crude has blown apart. That difference matters because oil is not diesel.

You cannot pour crude oil into a combine. You cannot put a barrel of Brent into a tractor.

Crude has to reach a refinery. The refinery has to have the capacity to process it. Diesel then has to reach the farm. The tractor has to run. The combine has to run. The crop has to be harvested. Grain has to be hauled. Food has to be processed. Refrigeration has to run. Trucks have to carry that food to distribution centers and grocery stores.

Energy is embedded in every step between the field and the consumer.

AGRICULTURE RUNS ON DIESEL.

The corn being harvested today is being harvested with machinery burning today's extraordinarily expensive diesel. That cost is entering the food system now. If diesel falls tomorrow, the farmer does not get today's fuel bill back. The fuel has already been burned.

Then that corn moves. Some becomes livestock feed. Some enters food manufacturing. Some becomes ethanol and other products. It has to be loaded, transported, dried, stored and processed. Every time it moves, energy enters the equation again.

THE PRICE OF DIESEL CAN FALL TOMORROW. THE HUMAN COST IT CREATES DOES NOT FALL WITH IT.

That is what I believe the market is missing. The market can trade the expectation that crude will fall. It can trade the expectation that the war will end. It can trade the expectation that more supply will come online. An algorithmic trading bot can reprice an oil future in milliseconds when a headline changes. A farmer cannot do that.

Agriculture works in seasons. If diesel is extraordinarily expensive during planting, the cost enters the crop before it emerges from the ground. If diesel is extraordinarily expensive during harvest, the cost enters while the crop is being removed from the field. Then the crop still has to be dried, stored, processed, transported and delivered.

The price on the screen can reverse. The cost already embedded in the crop cannot.

THE DIESEL SHOCK DOES NOT END AT THE DIESEL PUMP.

It moves into the crop. It moves into the farm balance sheet. It moves into transportation. It moves into processing. It moves into distribution. Eventually, it reaches the grocery store. And then another growing season begins.

Now add El Niño

Diesel would scare me enough by itself. It is not by itself. Now add an El Niño trend.

Agriculture is biological production sitting on top of an industrial supply chain. That means the farmer can absorb all of the industrial problems—fuel, fertilizer, machinery, transportation and financing—and still lose yield because it did not rain, because it rained too much, because the heat arrived at the wrong stage of plant development, because planting was delayed, or because harvest was delayed.

Weather does not care what the futures market priced yesterday.

The interview that prompted me to write this connected the same pressures: diesel demand rising during harvest, higher diesel feeding into grain production costs, grains feeding into biofuels, and multiple commodity markets beginning to move together. It also raised the danger of weather reducing yields while grain supplies are simultaneously being pressured by geopolitical disruptions.

That combination is what scares me. A reduction in yield is not a temporary red candle on a chart. Those bushels do not magically appear when the price changes.

A GROWING SEASON ONLY HAPPENS ONCE.

The market sees wheat. I see the crop. The market sees diesel. I see the tractor, the combine, the irrigation pump, the fertilizer truck and the truck hauling grain away from the farm. The market sees a reduction in yield. I see acres that cannot be manufactured after the growing season is over.

These risks do not politely wait for one another to end. They compound.

Now add war

Then put war on top of it. The global food system depends on transportation corridors, ports, refineries, shipping lanes, fertilizer production, energy supplies and grain-exporting regions scattered across the world. Disrupt one and the system has to compensate. Disrupt several at once and compensation becomes harder.

The discussion that prompted this article pointed to the Strait of Hormuz, the Red Sea, attacks affecting Black Sea oil infrastructure, refining constraints, transportation problems and grain-export routes. These are not separate stories when you are looking at agriculture.

Oil affects diesel. Diesel affects farming. War affects shipping. Shipping affects fertilizer and grain. Grain affects livestock. Energy affects processing. Transportation affects everything.

THEY COMPOUND.

That is the part a financial market built around individual tickers can make easy to miss. The farm does not experience these problems as individual tickers. The farm gets the bill for all of them.

And we have been weakening the system built to protect agriculture

Now add another problem. At the same time agriculture is confronting extraordinary energy costs, weather risk and geopolitical disruption, the federal agricultural system designed to support farmers, conduct research, monitor disease, collect agricultural information and protect the food supply has been undergoing major disruption of its own.

Thousands of USDA employees have left the department. Agricultural research, statistical collection, regional food programs and parts of the infrastructure surrounding animal-disease surveillance and food safety have faced staffing cuts, reorganizations or program changes.

The administration says its USDA restructuring is intended to reduce bureaucracy, lower costs and move services closer to farmers. But agriculture does not measure resilience by the size of an organizational chart. It measures resilience by what is still standing when something goes wrong.

Agricultural research is resilience. Animal-disease surveillance is resilience. Food-safety science is resilience. Accurate crop information is resilience. Regional processing and distribution are resilience. Experienced people are resilience.

People who understand crops, soils, livestock, disease, markets and agricultural systems matter most when conditions stop behaving normally.

RESILIENCE IS WHAT YOU BUILD BEFORE THE EMERGENCY.

You do not discover that you needed it after the emergency begins.

The market is looking in the wrong direction

Wall Street is watching oil. I am watching diesel. The market is watching whether crude falls back below a particular number. I am watching what the fuel already burned at today's prices is doing to the cost of producing food. Those are not the same thing.

Financial markets are extraordinarily good at pricing expectations. Autotrading bots, CTA models, momentum algorithms, NLP trading systems and execution engines can react to a new headline almost instantly. Agriculture cannot.

A president can change a statement in seconds. The Federal Reserve can change an interest rate in an afternoon. An algorithm can change the price of a commodity in milliseconds. A crop cannot rewind.

THE MARKET CAN REPRICE OIL IN MILLISECONDS. AGRICULTURE CANNOT REPLANT LAST SEASON.

That creates an enormous disconnect between the financial market and the physical economy. The market can begin pricing cheaper energy before cheaper energy arrives. The market can begin pricing peace before the physical disruptions of war have disappeared. The market can price lower inflation before the expensive crop has even reached the grocery store.

Agriculture has to pay the actual bill.

The cost has already entered the system

This is the point I do not think is being priced adequately. The fuel is being burned now. The money is being spent now. The crops are being harvested now. The transportation costs are being incurred now. Those costs do not disappear because crude falls next week.

WE ARE PAST THE POINT WHERE FIXING OIL FIXES WHAT HAS ALREADY HAPPENED.

That does not mean the future cannot improve. It means improvement tomorrow cannot erase a production cost incurred yesterday. And eventually another crop has to be planted.

Farmers make decisions about acreage, seed, fertilizer, equipment, financing and risk based on the economics they are living with now. A severe cost shock can therefore move beyond one harvest and into decisions surrounding the next growing season.

This is why I keep coming back to diesel. Diesel moves into the farm, the harvest, transportation, processing, distribution, the grocery store and finally the consumer. The diesel price can fall before the cost reaches the consumer. That lag is the danger.

Now put this on top of an economic crash

This is what scares me most. Imagine an agricultural cost shock occurring at the same time as a financial and economic downturn.

A financial crash destroys household wealth. An economic crash destroys jobs and income. Then the food system demands a larger share of whatever income remains.

Less money coming in. More money required to eat.

A family can postpone buying a car. It can cancel a vacation. It can keep the old television. It can stop eating at restaurants. It can wait another year to replace the furniture. Human beings need to eat.

Food is different. And when the cost of food rises sharply while income and wealth are falling, the problem moves beyond agriculture, beyond inflation and beyond Wall Street. It enters every kitchen in America. It enters every family budget. It enters every school cafeteria, restaurant, hospital, nursing home and food bank. It enters wages, corporate margins and government spending. Eventually, it enters the stability of the society itself.

A FINANCIAL CRISIS TAKES WEALTH. AN ECONOMIC CRISIS TAKES INCOME. A FOOD CRISIS REACHES BENEATH BOTH OF THEM.

That is why this scares me more than a market crash. The market can recover. The economy can be rebuilt. But the corn being harvested today only gets harvested once. The diesel being burned to harvest it only gets paid for once. The weather that produced it cannot be rerun. The yield that was lost cannot be manufactured in December.

And the people waiting at the other end of that system still have to eat.

THE MARKET TRADES THE EXPECTATION. THE FARM PAYS THE ACTUAL BILL.

And eventually the grocery store hands that bill to us.
