He simultaneously warned that hotter August inflation could justify a hike.

To a human reader, that is a conditional argument.

5. Warsh is telling the market to look at the building

Six days before Waller's speech, Chairman Kevin Warsh laid out a radically different philosophy of central-bank communication.

Warsh said the Federal Reserve needs market signals that are “as unfiltered as possible”: Treasury prices and trading volumes, the dollar, credit conditions, commodities and the internal information carried by markets themselves.

He warned against a market that looks primarily to the Federal Reserve for its next trade.

His warning was a hall of mirrors: the market watches the Fed, the Fed watches the market, and the resulting price can stop reflecting the economic information both sides are supposed to be reading.

WARSH IS TRYING TO MAKE THE MARKET LOOK AT THE BUILDING.

Look at oil.

Look at inflation.

Look at the price of money.

Look at Treasury yields.

Look at credit.

Look at the physical economy underneath the index.

Warsh's message is not subtle: market participants should draw their own conclusions from real economic information rather than waiting for the central bank to supply the next trade.

When long yields scream higher, the market is transmitting information about inflation, fiscal supply, borrowing costs, risk and the price of money.

7. Then Waller put another relief signal in front of the exit

Waller did exactly what Warsh had just warned can distort the relationship between the Fed and markets: he gave the market a reaction function.

Waller explicitly said that if the data arrive one way, he will advocate one policy path; if they arrive another way, he will advocate another.

Warsh had addressed that exact idea at Jackson Hole. He rejected the notion that a reaction function can provide a mechanical answer reliable enough for markets to follow, warning that it works better in theory than in practice.

Waller disagreed. He defended giving markets the parameters of his policy “strike zone.”

That disagreement is no longer academic when machines are reading every word.
